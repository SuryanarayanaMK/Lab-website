Teaching

Fall 2026: EN.553.413/613: Applied Statistics and Data Science. An applied introduction to probability and statistical learning as a coherent framework for learning from finite, noisy data. The course opens with probability foundations: sample spaces, counting, conditioning, independence, Bayes' rule, and random variables. It then develops sampling theory through the Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem toward confidence intervals, bootstrap methods, hypothesis testing, and randomized experiments. It continues with parameter estimation via maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference, then builds toward statistical modeling: linear regression, diagnostics, generalization and cross-validation, model selection, and regularization. The course closes with logistic regression, flexible and nonlinear regression, and generalized linear models. Concepts are developed throughout using real datasets, with two mid-term exams, project presentations, and a final exam.

  • Code: Lecture 9 Companion Notebooks Maximum Likelihood Estimation: A Visual Introduction: builds intuition for MLE on simulated Poisson counts by treating the data as fixed and the parameter as variable, plotting the likelihood, log-likelihood, and negative log-likelihood to show that all are optimized at λ̂ = x̄; then shows how the likelihood sharpens as n grows and simulates repeated experiments to recover the sampling distribution of the MLE with standard error √(λ/n). MLE with Real Financial Data: S&P 500 Returns: downloads daily S&P 500 closing prices from FRED, converts them to daily log-returns, and fits a Gaussian model by maximum likelihood to estimate the mean return and daily volatility; compares the fitted density with the empirical distribution to expose heavy tails, computes the standard error of the mean, and uses a parametric bootstrap to quantify uncertainty in the estimated volatility, emphasizing that MLE finds the best parameters within a chosen model but does not validate the model itself. Bootstrap vs. Analytic Uncertainty: Two Real-Data Examples: compares analytic standard errors with bootstrap estimates on two historical datasets: Michelson’s 1879 speed-of-light measurements (Gaussian model, sample mean with SE = S/√n) and Prussian cavalry horse-kick deaths over 280 corps-years (Poisson model, rate with SE = √(λ/n), checked against the exact fitted sampling distribution), overlaying the bootstrap distribution on the analytic one in each case.

  • Lecture 10 Develops the large-sample properties of maximum likelihood estimators: consistency, asymptotic normality with variance governed by the Fisher information, and the Cramér–Rao lower bound, showing that the MLE is asymptotically efficient; then moves from maximum likelihood to Bayesian inference, introducing priors and posteriors through Bayes’ rule, MAP estimation as likelihood plus prior information (illustrated on a small-sample Bernoulli coin), and the full posterior distribution as a description of the remaining uncertainty about the parameter.

  • Lecture 11 Develops Bayesian updating as posterior ∝ likelihood × prior and shows that sequential updating, in which each posterior becomes the prior for the next observation, gives the same result as processing the full dataset at once; introduces conjugate priors through the Beta–Binomial model, where updating reduces to counting heads and tails and the posterior concentrates as coin flips accumulate; applies Bayes’ rule to hypothesis testing between two competing coin models; and closes with the advantages and limitations of Bayesian inference: sensitivity to and choice of the prior, and the computational cost of the normalizing integral.

Bayesian Coin-Flip Inference: recreates the lecture’s coin-flip examples: sequential Bayesian hypothesis testing between H1: θ = 0.5 and H2: θ = 0.7 on simulated fair-coin flips, with each posterior becoming the next prior; sequential Beta–Binomial updating from a uniform Beta(1, 1) prior, tracking the posterior mean and standard deviation as flips accumulate; and a three-heads example contrasting the MLE θ̂ = 1 with the posterior under an informative prior.

  • Lecture 12 Moves from estimating a mean to estimating a conditional mean: after a primer on multivariate random variables and the multivariate Gaussian, shows that under squared-error loss the best predictor of Y given X = x is the regression function E[Y | X = x]; introduces simple linear regression and derives the ordinary least-squares estimates from the normal equations; shows that least squares is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise; rewrites the model in matrix form, y = Xβ + ε, giving the least-squares solution β̂ = (XTX)−1XTy and its extension to multiple linear regression; and shows that the least-squares estimator is unbiased with covariance σ2(XTX)−1.

  • Code: Lecture 12 Companion Notebooks Multivariate Distributions to Regression: uses the Iris data to move from one variable to a random vector, with joint distributions, mean vectors, covariance matrices, and pairwise plots; then uses the Palmer Penguins data to show how switching from describing the joint distribution to predicting one variable from another leads to conditional expectation and the simplest linear conditional model. Visualizing a 2D Multivariate Gaussian: builds intuition for the multivariate Gaussian primer by plotting a bivariate normal density as the covariance matrix changes: starting from an isotropic Gaussian, changing each variance separately, introducing positive and negative covariance, comparing several covariance matrices side by side, contrasting covariance with correlation, drawing covariance ellipses from the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of Σ, and shifting the mean without changing the covariance. Linear Regression on Palmer Penguins: predicts body mass from flipper length, starting from a single prediction for every penguin and moving to an empirical view of E[Y | X = x]; fits the least-squares line, examines the residuals, shows that the Gaussian MLE gives least squares, and colors the data by species to show that some apparent noise is variation from omitted variables. Linear Regression on Diabetes Data: uses the scikit-learn diabetes dataset to predict one-year disease progression from baseline BMI, comparing the fitted line with ignoring BMI, examining what remains after using X, and bootstrapping the slope. Multiple Linear Regression on Diabetes Data: extends from BMI alone to BMI, blood pressure, and age, and then to all baseline predictors, in matrix form; compares simple and multiple regression coefficients, computes standard errors and confidence intervals, visualizes coefficient uncertainty, discusses multicollinearity, and compares prediction error.
